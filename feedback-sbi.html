Constructive Performance Management: Applying the Situation-Behaviour-Impact (SBI) Model

In high-pressure industrial workspaces, engineering leads frequently encounter performance issues, technical errors, and communication failures. Whether addressing a draughtsman's repeated failure to implement Building Information Modeling (BIM) data loops or correcting a junior analyst's spreadsheet checking habits, vague criticism causes defensiveness and obscures solutions. The Center for Creative Leadership developed the **Situation-Behaviour-Impact (SBI) Model** to address this risk. It changes feedback from a subjective debate into an objective, factual alignment tool, making it perfect for demonstrating compliance with **UK-SPEC Competence C (Leadership)** and **Competence D (Communication)**.

The fundamental value of the SBI architecture lies in its ability to isolate an individual's personal identity from technical performance gaps. Traditional, unscripted feedback tends to focus on character traits (e.g., "you are being careless with design checks"), which breaks trust and violates the principles of psychological safety. The SBI framework resolves this by forcing the manager to structure the discussion across three distinct, scannable dimensions. First, anchor the feedback to a specific time and location (**Situation**). Second, isolate the precise, observable action or omission without judging personality (**Behaviour**). Third, outline the direct, data-driven cost, safety, or timeline outcomes caused by that action (**Impact**). This methodical layout creates a transparent audit trail for corporate continuous professional development (CPD) logs and review panels.

Deconstructing the Three Dimensions of an SBI Record

Constructing an objective personnel evaluation using the interactive SBI workspace requires technical managers to critically parse workplace events step-by-step:

Situation [S]: Define the exact timeline and spatial parameters of the event. Technical leaders must ground the feedback in a verifiable context to prevent ambiguity. For example, instead of stating "recently at work," write: "During Tuesday morning's hazop review session for the high-pressure pipeline layout module..." This prevents debate about when or where the issue occurred.

Behaviour [B]: Document the specific, observable actions or words that occurred. Managers must completely remove emotional adjectives and character judgements. Avoid words like "lazy," "unprofessional," or "distracted." Instead, stick to raw facts: e.g., "You did not supply the updated transient flow calculation sheets, and you left the review meeting 15 minutes before formal risk sign-off was completed."

Impact [I]: Translate the observed behaviour into measurable project, financial, or safety consequences. This step is vital for demonstrating commercial awareness under UK-SPEC guidelines. Clearly document the real-world outcomes: e.g., "This omission halted the hazard review loop, caused a 48-hour delay in our design submittal, and exposed the firm to a £15,000 contractual milestone penalty from the client."
