Reflective Leadership and Project Recovery: The SHARE Model

As technical candidates advance into principal engineering, program management, or corporate operations roles, their day-to-day challenges shift away from siloed mathematics. Senior leadership requires navigating unpredictable project bottlenecks, managing vendor resource shortages, and mitigating geopolitical supply chain delays. While basic behavioral tools like STAR map simple operations well, they frequently omit the specific external frictions that disrupt execution. The **SHARE Model (Situation, Hindrance, Action, Results, Evaluation)** expands on standard behavioral reporting. It introduces formal barrier tracking and retrospective loops, satisfying **UK-SPEC Competence D (Communication)** and **Competence E (Commitment)**.

The primary benefit of utilizing the SHARE methodology lies in the explicit mapping of the **Hindrance** phase. Review panels from professional institutions look for proof of resilience and adaptive risk management. By clearly separating a baseline project environment from unexpected commercial or physical barriers, candidates can document exactly how they recovered a failing asset program. Furthermore, the framework integrates a comprehensive **Evaluation** block. This guarantees that your technical portfolio goes beyond simple, passive event logging to present deep reflective learning, helping you secure professional accreditation.

Analyzing the Five Strategic Core Tiers of SHARE

Constructing an audit-ready, compliant SHARE evidence record requires engineers to critically analyze project recoveries step-by-step:

Situation [S]: This initial tier establishes the project environment, initial design parameters, scheduling thresholds, or asset requirements. Candidates must define the scope cleanly, embedding financial baselines, resource parameters, and standard compliance specifications (such as Eurocodes, ASME, or ISO guidelines) to provide solid commercial context.

Hindrance [H]: The hindrance quadrant identifies the sudden commercial barrier, technical defect, or supply chain bottleneck that disrupted execution. Rather than stating "the project was delayed," define the specific risk parameters: e.g. "An international vendor declared insolvencies, blocking the delivery of specialized cast turbine housings, threatening a 14-week program expansion, and risking a £2M late-delivery contractual penalty."

Action [A]: The action segment captures your personal technical and leadership contributions during the crisis. Explain what alternative first-principles calculations you ran, what script parameters you modified, what compliance frameworks you deployed, and how you managed cross-functional team networks to isolate or bypass the identified hindrance safely.

Results [R]: This section documents the data-driven milestones achieved through your technical actions. Review boards require clear empirical proof of project recovery. Entries must quantify optimized performance parameters, validated safety clearances, minimized expenditure losses, or formal regulatory sign-offs under relevant CDM governance frameworks.

Evaluation [E]: The final tier represents the reflective analysis required for professional registration. Authors must evaluate what the crisis revealed about system vulnerabilities. This involves documenting how this experience optimized subsequent engineering workflows, updated material procurement criteria, or revised institutional codes of practice to prevent recurrent liabilities.
