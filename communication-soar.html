The SOAR Technique: Mastering Behavioural Interviews for Engineering Leaders

Navigating the professional path toward registration as a Chartered Engineer (CEng) or Incorporated Engineer (IEng) culminates in the definitive Professional Review Interview (PRI). During this intensive assessment, review panels from licensed institutions do not simply look for basic competency boxes; they actively interrogate the candidate's psychological maturity, risk navigation frameworks, and ethical alignment. While the standard STAR framework is widely known, the advanced **SOAR Technique (Situation, Opportunity, Action, Result)** shifts the focus from passive task execution to proactive value innovation. This model is engineered to satisfy the high-level criteria of **UK-SPEC Competence D**, proving that an engineer can communicate value effectively across complex multi-disciplinary domains.

The core advantage of utilizing the SOAR framework lies in the replacement of the rigid "Task" parameter with the forward-looking **Opportunity** phase. In senior operations, a technical problem is never merely an assigned duty; it represents a strategic opportunity to optimize a workflow, eliminate structural waste, insulate an organization from risk, or introduce breakthrough engineering methodologies. By restructuring your behavioral interview narratives around this specific lens, you demonstrate to institutional assessors that your leadership satisfies Competence C (Management) and Competence D (Communication). It proves that your decision-making aligns micro-environmental constraints directly with long-term corporate sustainability and capital efficiency targets.

Deconstructing the Four Quadrants of the SOAR Framework

Constructing a professional engineering evidence block using the SOAR workspace requires a meticulous balance of technical context and commercial acumen:

Situation [S]: This quadrant defines the baseline project status, physical constraints, operational parameters, or systemic breakdowns. Candidates must avoid vague storytelling by hardcoding exact values: e.g., structural failure thresholds, supply chain expansion delays, or regulatory compliance liabilities. This sets a clear baseline for your upcoming decisions.

Opportunity [O]: The opportunity phase represents the primary point of differentiation in the SOAR method. Here, you isolate how the problem statement was leveraged to introduce systemic optimization. Instead of just fixing a component, explain how you identified a wider opportunity to deploy automated calculation macros, implement lean parameters, or rewrite standard asset configurations to prevent recurrent failures.

Action [A]: The action block captures your specific technical and leadership contributions. You must detail what *you* personally calculated, simulated, authorized, or designed. Document the application of first-principles mathematics, the optimization of script layers, the coordination of hazard reviews, and your management of multi-disciplinary teams. Use strong engineering verbs to clearly isolate your accountability.

Result [R]: The final quadrant provides the empirical proof of success. Review panels will dismiss entries that rely on generic qualitative statements. Candidates must hardcode verifiable data-driven results, such as percentage capacity gains, quantified capital expenditure optimization, life-cycle carbon reductions, or formal regulatory sign-offs under relevant CDM frameworks.
